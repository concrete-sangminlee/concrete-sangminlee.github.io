# Teaching Statement

Sang Min Lee · Ph.D. Candidate in Artificial Intelligence, Seoul National University

Engineering students increasingly need two kinds of literacy at once: how structures behave, and how data and models behave. I have taught both. Since 2021 I have served as a teaching assistant for eight courses at Seoul National University, from mechanics of materials, structural experiments, and prestressed concrete to MATLAB, Arduino and embedded-systems labs, and deep neural networks. I was head TA for *Introduction to IoT, AI, and Big Data* in Fall 2022, the semester the College of Engineering gave me its Outstanding Teaching Assistant Award. Before graduate school, I taught mathematics for a year and a half to students from low-income families with Edushare Korea, first as a teacher and then as lead teacher of its Uiwang Bugok center. My goal as a teacher is to graduate engineers who can use data-driven tools *and* judge when not to trust them.

## Principles

**Build, predict, measure.** Students understand an equation when they have predicted a result with it and then watched reality agree or disagree. In *Structural Experiment and Material*, I ran experiments on Euler buckling, truss deflection, and seismic design on a two-way shake table, in which students compared measured response with their calculations. In three IoT courses, I ran labs on Arduino UNO, ESP32, Teensy, and STM boards, so that sensing and control were things students made work. In *Architecture and AI*, each spring from 2022 to 2024, I led hands-on sessions in which students trained deep neural networks themselves before we discussed why the networks behaved as they did. I will keep this order in every course I teach: prediction first, then measurement, then explanation.

**A low barrier and a high standard.** Many architecture and civil engineering students arrive believing that programming or machine learning is not for them. In MATLAB labs for *Computation in Architectural Engineering* and problem sessions for *Mechanics of Materials for Architectural Engineering*, I broke problems into steps small enough to start on, then asked students to justify every step rather than just reach the answer. Edushare Korea taught me the same thing from the other side: a student who has fallen behind needs a first success quickly and a clear path from there, and low expectations do them no favors.

**Judgment is part of the subject.** My research is about making models dependable with little failure data, few sensors, and safety-critical codes, and I bring those questions into the classroom. Whenever students build a model, I ask them to state what it has never seen, how it would fail, and what an engineer should check before acting on it. Managing *Prestressed Concrete*, with its focus on post-tensioned structures, showed me how much design rests on understanding assumptions and limits. Data-driven tools deserve the same scrutiny, and students should practice giving it.

## Assessment

I assess what I want students to be able to do. Having evaluated student IoT projects across three courses, I have seen that a working demo can hide shallow understanding, so in my courses projects will be graded on a short written justification and an oral check alongside the result: why this sensor, why this model, what would make it wrong. Labs will begin with a written prediction, so that the gap between prediction and measurement, not just the measurement, is what students reflect on. I will collect anonymous feedback at mid-semester, not only at the end, and adjust while the course is still running.

## Courses

I am prepared to teach, in Korean or English:

- **Machine learning for civil and structural engineering**, undergraduate or graduate;
- structural health monitoring and non-destructive testing;
- data-driven wind engineering;
- programming, sensing, and IoT for engineers;
- foundational courses in structural mechanics and concrete structures.

The first course I would develop is *Machine Learning for Infrastructure*, organized around the scarcities engineers actually face, with each module built on public data that my own research has used:

| Weeks | Topic | Lab |
|---|---|---|
| 1–3 | Signals and features from structures | Classify impact-echo responses from multi-organization open data |
| 4–6 | Learning from normal behavior | Anomaly detection on public shake-table data |
| 7–9 | Sparse sensing and reconstruction | Recover wind pressures from a reduced set of taps |
| 10–11 | Models for the field | Fit a classifier within the limits of an edge device |
| 12–13 | Language models and building codes | Retrieval over code clauses that differ by one condition |
| 14–16 | Team project | From sensor to model to an engineering decision, with a written risk assessment |

## Mentoring

I began research as an undergraduate assistant in three different labs, working on wind pressure prediction, decision analysis for improving military dining facilities, and an IoT device for detecting condensation that I designed and built. I know what a first research project needs: a question small enough to finish, a result the student owns, and someone who checks in often. As a Ph.D. student I have worked closely with labmates on papers they led, on deep SVDD for monitoring and on impact-echo testing behind containment liner plates. In my own group, I will give every student a first-authored project with a clear scope, weekly one-on-one meetings, and practice in writing and presenting from the first year. I will measure my mentoring by whether students leave able to frame a question, design an experiment that could prove them wrong, and explain the result to an engineer who has to act on it.
